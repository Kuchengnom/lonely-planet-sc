There is a city on Pyro V that shouldn't exist. It sits on the polar plateau, roughly 30 kilometers from the Pole Station, and it is made entirely of ice — not just covered in ice, but constructed from it, as if someone took a block of pure glacial ice and carved it into a city.

The Frozen City has no records of construction. No launch signatures. No industrial activity detected by orbital sensors. It simply is. And it is the single most mysterious structure in the entire Stanton system.

## Getting There

The Frozen City is accessible from Pole Station, but the journey is not trivial. The polar plateau is subject to extreme weather, and the city itself sits in a zone of unusual thermal activity that makes navigation difficult.

**From Pole Station:**

1. Deploy from Pole Station's exterior hangar (requires thermal suit exit protocol)
2. Engage surface transit vehicle — standard rovers are insufficient; use a tracked, heated vehicle
3. Travel northeast at approximately 40 km/h (surface conditions vary; expect 45–60 minutes)
4. The city's thermal signature becomes visible on suit HUD at approximately 15 km range
5. The final approach requires navigation through ice formations and wind-scoured ridges
6. Land at the city's perimeter wall — there are no open entrances, only narrow doorways

**Alternative — Orbital Drop:**

1. Quantum to Pyro V orbit (coordinates available from RSI Spectrum community)
2. Atmospheric entry at high angle (65°+) to minimize heating and maintain control
3. Glide to the polar plateau using thermals (the city sits in a persistent wind corridor)
4. Land on the plateau 2 km from the perimeter — approach on foot through the ice field

<TouristTip>
  The single most important rule of visiting the Frozen City: never go alone. The structures are unstable in ways that aren't obvious until something falls on you, and no one will hear you call for help. The thermal anomalies mean that suit heaters can fail at any time, and the ice is so reflective that a wrong turn in a blizzard sends you into disorientation. If your suit heater dies while you're lost in an ice field, you will not survive. Bring a second person. Bring a satellite phone. Bring an axe.
</TouristTip>

## What You'll Find

The Frozen City is not large — perhaps two square kilometers of structure arranged in a roughly circular pattern around a central plaza. It consists of approximately 200 individual structures, all carved from ice with architectural precision that rivals any city in human space.

**The Central Plaza:** At the heart of the city is a large open plaza, approximately 500 meters across, with a single feature: a sculpture. It is made of the same ice as the surrounding buildings, but it is clearly not natural. It is approximately 15 meters tall, shaped like a spiraling tower with geometric patterns carved into its surface. The patterns are mathematical — not decorative, but deliberate, as if someone encoded information into the ice.

**The Residential Districts:** The outer regions of the city consist of what appear to be residential structures — small, uniform buildings arranged in rows. Each is approximately 10 meters square and 8 meters tall. The doorways are all the same size: 2 meters wide, 3 meters tall. There are no windows.

**The Technical Structures:** Between the residential blocks and the central plaza are larger structures with more complex geometries. These appear to be technical or ceremonial in function — some have internal chambers that are still accessible, though the thermal conditions inside make prolonged stays impossible. The internal temperatures in these chambers can drop to -180°C or lower, even on the coldest day.

**The Perimeter Wall:** The city is enclosed by a wall approximately 4 meters high and 2 meters thick, made of compressed ice. There are four gateways in the wall, each 5 meters wide, but they appear to have been sealed at some point. The ice at the gateways has a different texture — it looks like it was melted and refrozen, as if the city was designed to be isolated from the outside world.

## The Mystery

The Frozen City's origin is unknown. Several theories circulate among the Stanton exploration community:

1. **Pre-human construction**: Some speculate that the city predates human arrival in the Pyro system, built by some unknown civilization. The mathematical patterns on the central sculpture suggest non-human intelligence.

2. **Experimental ArcCorp project**: Others suggest that ArcCorp built the city as an experimental structure — a test of extreme-environment construction techniques. There is no documentation of such a project, but ArcCorp's record-keeping is notoriously incomplete.

3. **Temporal anomaly**: A fringe theory suggests that the city was "imported" from somewhere else — another time, another place. The ice shows no signs of age; it is as fresh as if it were carved yesterday. Some explorers report that the city's geometry appears to shift slightly when viewed from different angles, as if the structure is not entirely three-dimensional.

4. **AI construction**: A more grounded theory proposes that an autonomous construction AI built the city during a period of Pyro system activity, then abandoned it when the mission parameters changed.

None of these theories have been verified. None of them explain why the city sits on a planet where nothing of any kind is supposed to survive for more than a few minutes outside a pressurized environment.

## Photographic Opportunities

The Frozen City is perhaps the most photographically compelling location in the Pyro system — and possibly in the entire Stanton system. Every angle offers something new, and the interplay of ice, light, and geometry creates images that defy the eye's expectations.

**Wide-angle cityscapes**: The circular layout of the city creates a striking composition against the flat polar plateau. Photograph from any elevated position — the perimeter wall, the central sculpture, or a nearby ridge — to capture the full extent of the structure.

**Close-up architectural details**: The ice surfaces are carved with precision. Focus on the mathematical patterns in the central sculpture, the uniformity of the residential structures, and the texture variations in the perimeter wall.

**Light and shadow**: The red star's low-angle light creates dramatic shadows across the ice. Golden hour on Pyro V (which occurs for approximately 4 hours per 30-hour day) is the best time for photography, when the light is warm enough to reveal the ice's blue-green tones.

**Thermal imaging**: The city's thermal anomalies create fascinating thermal photographs. The central sculpture and the technical structures show distinct thermal signatures that differ from the natural ice. A thermal camera is not just a safety tool — it's a photographic tool.

<TouristTip>
  Bring polarizing filters. The ice reflects the red star's light so intensely that a polarizer is essential for controlling glare. Without one, your images will be washed out and your eyes will ache from looking at the surface. The best polarizing shots come from approaching the central sculpture from the west at golden hour, when the light catches the geometric patterns at their steepest angle.
</TouristTip>

## Safety Considerations

- **Thermal exposure**: The polar plateau temperature can drop to -150°C or lower. Even with proper suits, prolonged exposure is dangerous. Limit surface stays to 6 hours maximum.
- **Structural stability**: The ice structures are under constant stress from thermal cycling and wind. Do not touch, climb, or lean against any structure. The central sculpture in particular has shown signs of instability in recent visits.
- **Navigation**: The ice field surrounding the city has no landmarks. GPS signals are unreliable due to the ice's interference with satellite navigation. Always carry a compass, printed maps, and an emergency beacon.
- **Communication blackout**: The polar plateau's ice creates a communication shadow zone. Inform Pole Station of your planned surface time before departing. If you don't check in, they will assume you've been lost — and rescue takes time.
- **Ice thickness**: The frozen methane lakes near the city have variable thickness. Test ice before walking on it with an axe or ice pick. Several pilots have fallen through thin ice and required rescue.
