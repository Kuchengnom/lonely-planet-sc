Pyro I is the first planet from the star Pyro, at a distance of just 8.2 gigameters. It is, by any reasonable measure, a terrible place to exist. The surface temperature is approximately 220°C. The atmosphere is toxic. There is nothing on the surface that anyone would reasonably want — no significant mineral deposits, no water, no habitable zones. The planet is charred black rock that is slowly being incorporated into the star itself, its outer layers gradually eroded by the constant bombardment of stellar flares.

And yet, if you can survive long enough to look at Pyro I from the safety of its Lagrangian points, you will see something that most pilots in the verse will never witness: two violent distortion storms, churning in a planet's atmosphere at temperatures that should not support atmospheric physics, surrounded by irradiation levels that would kill an unprotected human in minutes.

## The Distortion Storms

Two violent distortion storms occupy the near-orbital space of Pyro I. These are not weather phenomena in any conventional sense — they are regions of extreme spatial distortion, where the fundamental properties of space-time are warped by the combined effects of intense stellar radiation, the planet's own magnetic field, and whatever geological processes are driving this planet's perpetual motion toward dissolution.

The storms are described in the wiki as having "lethal irradiated conditions" within them. This is an understatement. The irradiation levels inside the distortion storms are not merely dangerous — they are instantaneous. Exposure to the interior of either storm will destroy electronics, compromise shielding, and irradiate biological tissue to lethal levels before the ship's instruments can complete a single reading.

The storms are in constant motion, swirling around the planet's northern hemisphere (where a large permanent cyclone also exists) and occasionally extending into the southern hemisphere. Their exact boundaries are unpredictable and shifting — the distortion effects make reliable long-range measurement impossible.

### What They Look Like

From the L-point observation positions, the distortion storms appear as vast, churning regions of distorted light. The star Pyro, viewed through the storms, appears warped and stretched — the light bending around the extreme gravitational and electromagnetic fields within the storm zones. The storms themselves glow with an iridescent light, the result of ionized particles interacting with the stellar wind.

From a safe distance (L2 or L3), the storms can appear deceptively beautiful — vast, swirling vortices of color and light, moving in patterns that have no obvious logic. Up close, they are the most violent natural phenomena in the system, and being within their reach at anything less than maximum ship speed will result in catastrophic damage.

### The Permanent Cyclone

In addition to the two distortion storms, Pyro I hosts a large permanent cyclone in its northern hemisphere. This is not a weather system in the conventional sense — it is a persistent atmospheric vortex maintained by the extreme temperature differential between the planet's star-facing and anti-star-facing hemispheres. The cyclone rotates continuously, its winds reaching speeds that would strip paint from any ship caught in its path.

The cyclone and the distortion storms interact in unpredictable ways. The cyclone's rotation affects the boundaries of the storms, and the storms' distortion fields affect the cyclone's structure. The result is a chaotic atmospheric system that is impossible to model with any degree of accuracy.

## The Observation Points

You cannot observe the storms from the surface of Pyro I. The planet's surface temperature, toxic atmosphere, and the storms themselves make the surface a death sentence. The only way to observe the storms safely is from the Lagrangian points — the stable gravitational positions between Pyro I and its star where the PYAM-FARSTAT stations were originally built.

### PYR1 L1

The L1 point lies between Pyro I and the star. It offers the closest safe observation point of the distortion storms, but it is also the most dangerous, as it is the closest stable position to the storms' active zones. No PYAM station was built here in the original configuration, and the position offers limited long-term habitability due to the constant exposure to stellar radiation.

### PYR1 L2

The L2 point is located behind Pyro I relative to the star. A PYAM station was built here (designated PYAM-FARSTAT-1-2). This position provides an excellent view of the storms' dark side — where the storms are silhouetted against the star, creating a dramatic contrast that is particularly photogenic. However, the position is also exposed to the star's flare activity, as it is not protected by the planet's bulk.

### PYR1 L3

The L3 point is on the opposite side of the star from Pyro I. A PYAM station was built here (PYAM-FARSTAT-1-3), and it is also home to the Akiro Cluster (Pyro Cluster Alpha) — a collection of asteroids and debris that forms a natural barrier between the L3 point and the rest of the inner system. This position offers the safest observation of the storms (being furthest from their active zones), but the view is the most distant and least dramatic.

### PYR1 L4 and L5

L4 and L5 are the triangular Lagrangian points, located 60 degrees ahead of and behind Pyro I in its orbit. A PYAM station was built at L5 (PYAM-FARSTAT-1-5), but not at L4. These positions offer a side-on view of the storms, which is the least common observational perspective and therefore the most valuable for documentation purposes. The L4 position, being unoccupied, is the most exposed and the least defensible — it has no station infrastructure and no shelter.

## The Abandoned PYAM Stations

Five PYAM-FARSTAT stations were built at Pyro I's Lagrangian points during the company's operations in the system. By the time they were abandoned in the 2550s (before the final bankruptcy in 2563), all of these stations had been stripped of valuable equipment and left to drift. Some have since been claimed by scavengers and pirates, who use the abandoned infrastructure as a forward operating base for operations in the inner system.

The stations are largely derelict. The ones that have been partially reoccupied by scavengers retain basic life support and power systems, but they are not suitable for long-term habitation. The constant radiation and the proximity to the distortion storms make them undesirable as permanent bases — they are used more as waystations and hideouts than as homes.

## The View from L-Point

The combination of the distortion storms, the permanent cyclone, the irradiated atmosphere, and the star Pyro itself creates a sight that most pilots will never experience. From the L-point stations, Pyro I is a world actively being destroyed — a planet of black rock, toxic atmosphere, and violent storms, orbiting a violent star, slowly being consumed over millions of years.

The storms themselves are the main attraction. They are, in a word, beautiful. The way the light from Pyro bends through the distorted space, the way the ionized particles glow in colors that don't have names, the way the storms seem to move with a kind of deliberate violence — these are the kinds of things that people travel across the verse to see.

The price of admission is steep. The radiation, the heat, the distortion damage, the unpredictable storm boundaries — all of these will kill you. The L-point stations offer marginally better protection than being in open space, but "marginally" is the key word. You are still in the most dangerous part of the most dangerous system in the verse.
