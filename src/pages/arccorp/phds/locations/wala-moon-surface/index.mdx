Wala is what happens when a planet grows greedy. Originally a perfectly spherical moon approximately 800 km in diameter, ArcCorp's gravity has been slowly pulling on Wala for centuries — and the moon is fighting back. The result is a misshapen, elongated body that looks like it was squeezed between the fists of two indifferent gods.

This is not a place you visit for beauty. You visit Wala because it shouldn't exist, because the laws of orbital mechanics have been bent into something alien by the sheer mass of the world it orbits, and because standing on a moon that is literally being crushed by gravity is one of the most humbling experiences in human space.

## Getting There

Wala's orbit is unstable and heavily influenced by ArcCorp's gravitational field. You cannot fly straight to it — you need to navigate the same radiation belts that surround ArcCorp itself, then match Wala's highly elliptical orbital velocity.

**From Port Wallis:**

1. Jump to **ST-ARCCORP-VIA-1Q** (ArcCorp quantum gateway)
2. Re-quantum to **ST-ARCCORP-VIA-2Q** — this is the gateway closest to Wala's orbit
3. Drop out of QT at approximately **1,200 km from Wala's L4 point**
4. Match Wala's orbital velocity (approximately 14.7 km/s) using low-thrust burns
5. Approach from the sun-facing side to maintain visibility and avoid tidal force hotspots
6. Landing requires a ship with reinforced landing gear — the surface is uneven and the tidal forces make any attempt to hover dangerous

**Alternative OM Triangulation:**

- **OM-2**: 892.4 km | **OM-4**: 1,156.7 km | **OM-6**: 743.2 km
- Use these to triangulate your position once in Wala's vicinity. The moon's irregular shape means OM readings will vary significantly depending on approach angle.

<TouristTip>
  Do NOT attempt to land on Wala in a ship that hasn't been reinforced for tidal stress. The moon's gravity field is so distorted that even small ships have been torn apart by differential forces. If your ship wasn't built for deep-space industrial work, it's not built for Wala. The radiation belts are also a concern — ArcCorp's belt extends well beyond the planet's atmosphere and makes Wala's surface a dosimeter's worst nightmare.
</TouristTip>

## What You'll Find

Wala's surface is a landscape of impossibilities. The moon's elongation means that the "ends" of the moon experience significantly stronger tidal forces than the "middle," creating vast stretches of compressed rock near the poles and near-zero gravity zones along the equator.

**The Compressed Poles:** At the moon's elongated ends, you'll find surface formations that look like they've been pressed by an invisible hand. Mountains have been flattened into broad plateaus. Craters have been stretched into long, narrow trenches. The ground here is so compressed that it cracks under the weight of your own feet — don't walk too far from your ship.

**The Tidal Equator:** Running along Wala's midline is a region of near-zero gravity. Objects released here will float in slow, unpredictable orbits around the moon. The ground itself is covered in floating debris — rocks, boulders, and chunks of ice that have been pulled loose by the tidal forces and never fell back down. This is the most dangerous area for landing; you risk floating off into space if your anchor fails.

**The Gravity Tides:** Wala experiences gravity tides — periodic shifts in local gravity as it passes through different parts of ArcCorp's gravitational field. During peak tide events (every 18 ArcCorp hours), gravity can shift direction for brief moments. This is when the floating debris falls, creating rain of rock from the sky. Wait out peak tide events inside your ship.

## Photographic Opportunities

Wala offers no beauty in the traditional sense. It's a place of geological violence, and the photographs reflect that. The most compelling images come from emphasizing the scale of the deformation.

- **Wide-angle distortion shots**: Capture the moon's elongated shape against ArcCorp's backdrop. The contrast between the round planet and the stretched moon is striking and illustrates the tidal forces at work.
- **Floating debris**: Long-exposure shots of rocks and ice suspended in the tidal equator create surreal, dreamlike images. Use a tripod and 5–10 second exposures to capture the slow-motion chaos.
- **Compressed surface formations**: The flattened mountains and stretched craters are unique in the Stanton system. Close-up shots of the deformed ground reveal the incredible stress that created these features.
- **ArcCorp silhouette**: Photographing Wala with ArcCorp filling the frame creates a powerful narrative — the world that crushed its moon, framed against its own industrial sprawl.

<TouristTip>
  The most photographed aspect of Wala is the floating debris at the tidal equator. It looks impossible — boulders suspended in mid-air, rotating slowly in zero-g — and it is. The only way to capture it is with a tripod and long exposure. Do not hand-hold. The tidal forces will shake your camera, and you'll waste a perfect subject on motion blur.
</TouristTip>

## Safety Considerations

- **Tidal forces**: These are the primary danger. Never land in a zone where differential gravity exceeds 0.5g across your ship's length. Use your ship's onboard tidal force calculator to verify landing zones before committing.
- **Radiation**: Wala sits within ArcCorp's radiation belt. Even short surface stays accumulate dangerous doses. Limit surface time to 2 hours maximum without heavy shielding.
- **Floating debris**: In the tidal equator, debris floats at unpredictable velocities. Always wear a helmet and sealed suit. A floating boulder moving at 10 m/s will kill you as surely as a bullet.
- **Communication blackouts**: The tidal forces interfere with standard communication equipment. Inform your ship or station contact of your planned surface time before disembarking. If you don't check in, they'll assume you've been lost to the void.
